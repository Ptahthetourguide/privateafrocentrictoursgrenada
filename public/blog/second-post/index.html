Jab Jab the Journey

Emancipated but Still Not Free

Jab Jab After Slavery

Carnival as we know it today originated from the Roman celebration of Carna Vale, which French patois transformed into Carnival. Catholic planters explained this to their government as a Christian lesson of compassion — giving their slaves leftovers and one day off work each year to indulge, celebrate, and socialize on the plantations.

Emancipation came seventy‑three years after Grenada became a British colony in 1763 following the Treaty of Paris. This left a large number of Creolized Africans and mixed‑race French on the island. The Fedon revolt of 1795 reduced their numbers through fighting, executions, exile, and migration. The revolt left the island divided along national and religious lines. The emancipated Africans had a choice: Catholic or Anglican, French or British.

Evangelism and the introduction of the Slave Bible to the West Indies caused Grenadians to convert to Christianity and forget Anansi. Many chose Roman Catholicism as resistance to the Protestant British. Anansi’s festival, Carnival, created during French Catholic colonization, could only continue as a religious festival. Roman Catholic identity gave Africans leverage to make Carnival a day of celebration. Others became Anglican to gain representation under the British.

Religion complicated Jab Jab’s existence, demonizing the culture and turning it into a symbol of fear for Christians.

J’ouvert Morning Jab Jab Band

Carnival returned on Emancipation Day to celebrate the end of chattel slavery and became a celebration of freedom. Jab Jab appeared as individuals or small groups of masqueraders. Their costumes depicted the Biblical Devil with horns to the newly Christianized Africans. The culture was now seen as bad, evil, and worshiping the devil in his fiery kingdom. Fear caused Africans to convert to Christianity and forget Anansi.

Colonization made Africans capital in a capitalist economy. Ex‑slaves were thrown into this system with no capital of their own, because they themselves had been the capital. The only difference was that they knew their strength — one had to be strong to survive chattel slavery.

Jab Jab culture followed Grenadians and Carnival to numerous capitals and cities worldwide where Grenadians migrated. It was created on the ex‑slave plantation island of Grenada, inherited from a dispossessed people determined to survive through all generations. An African survivor, a Grenadian Jab.

Jab Jab Is the Devil

Early emancipation was a time of transition. Religion played a major role in destroying African spirituality by capturing the African mind. Once in control of the mind, bondage was no longer needed — fear could be injected directly. Properly applied, fear made Africans run from their spirituality, believing only the Christian God could save them.

Jab Jab declined rapidly as people flocked to churches. The Protestant Church of England’s role in the colonies was conversion. Worshiping the colonizers’ God gave Africans a false sense of equality. They became convinced Jab Jab was the Biblical devil, and terrible things awaited them if they worshiped him.

St.Georges J’ouvert Morning Jab Jab Culture

Emancipation Day 1834 marked the first Carnival in Grenada. In 1877 Grenada held its first official Carnival under British rule. Anansi’s temple was rebuilt, and Jab Jab reappeared publicly with the sound of the conch shell on J’ouvert morning, calling Grenadians to worship and play Mas.

Churches profited — and still profit — from slavery. The post‑emancipation era brought new challenges. Churches worked with government to shape laws and educate children. Schools were mainly faith‑based and ended for most at the primary level. Parents welcomed education they themselves had been denied, not realizing indoctrination was the agenda. Religion distracted children from spirituality, turning Anansi into a fairy tale.

Jab Jab Costume Is Black and Dirty

Freedom for an enslaved people could not be dressed up. Jab Jab had no costume because our ancestors were afforded at best two sets of garments. Everything — clothes, tools, even the slaves themselves — belonged to the master. Punishment awaited anyone who mishandled the master’s property.

Mud or charcoal covered their bodies, disguising them and washing off later. Today, used vehicle oil creates the black shine, but molasses was used when sugar cane dominated agriculture. That Jab Jab is called Jab Molassie. As Carnival developed internationally, colored Jab Jab bands emerged, but Grenada maintained black in keeping with tradition.

As dispossessed people, their only access was to discarded items the master allowed them to use. Symbolism and color painted the message: “This is the Devil.”

Early masqueraders made helmets from disused chamber pots with attached cow horns — the message: Do not put shit in your head. Red dye dripped from their mouths, symbolizing blood. The conch shell signaled wake up. The drums were the heartbeat of the ancestors, carried in the coffin with other spirits, especially the liquid intoxicating spirit. The chains symbolized what we must break every day to be free.

Some call it a Street Festival: We call it Playing Mas

Jab Jab and Grenada Carnival

Despite demonization, Jab Jab survived. It became the heartbeat of Grenada Carnival, celebrated every August. J’ouvert morning is the climax, when Grenadians take to the streets in oil, mud, and paint. The Jab Jab rhythm drives Soca music, inspiring generations of artists and performers.

Carnival is not just entertainment; it is a cultural archive. Each chant, drumbeat, and costume tells the story of survival, resistance, and joy. Jab Jab is the heartbeat of Carnival, reminding Grenadians of their African roots.

Jab Jab and Grenada Tours

Today, Grenada Tours offer visitors a chance to experience Jab Jab firsthand. Cultural packages include J’ouvert participation, storytelling sessions, and visits to historic sites. Tourists learn that Jab Jab is more than a masquerade — it is a living tradition, a spiritual practice, and a cultural inheritance.

By promoting Jab Jab through tourism, Grenadians share their story with the world while strengthening local identity. Jab Jab becomes both a cultural export and a reminder of resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why was Jab Jab demonized after emancipation? A: Religion played a major role in demonizing African spirituality. Churches taught that Jab Jab was the Biblical devil, instilling fear to control the population.

Q: What is Jab Malasie? A: Jab Malasie is a form of Jab Jab costume using molasses for the black shine, originating when sugar cane dominated Grenada’s agriculture.

Q: How did Jab Jab survive colonization? A: By disguising African spirituality within Carnival, Grenadians preserved their heritage under colonial rule. Jab Jab became both resistance and resilience.

Q: Can visitors experience Jab Jab today? A: Yes. Private AfroCentric Tours include J’ouvert morning celebrations, where visitors can join Jab Jab masqueraders in the streets.

Conclusion

Jab Jab’s journey after slavery is one of survival, adaptation, and resilience. Demonized by religion as Devil worship, denied the freedom of expression and thrown into capitalism without capital, Grenadians still preserved their culture. Jab Jab remains a living symbol of resistance, celebrated in Grenada Carnival and shared with the world through Private AfroCentric Tours Grenada.

Grenada Jab Jab is not the devil — it is the spirit of survival, resilience, and cultural pride.
