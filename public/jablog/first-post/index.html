Jab Jab: The Akan Spirit of Spicemas

Living Archaeology

The conch shell sounds as latecomers arrive for the pouring of old oil. Once everyone is blackened, they visit the coffin to release the intoxicating spirit — rum — and pour libation to invite the ancestors. As the first rays of sun appear, it is J’ouvert morning.

This is the moment Jab Jab lives for: to play Mas. The warm‑up begins with another drink of rum. Now come the important parts of every Jab Jab costume — the horns, chain, and red mouth. J’ouvert starts with the sun and a pulsating Jab Jab rhythm, leading into the call‑and‑response chant. The conch shell conducts the rhythm section of old kitchen utensils and anything that can hold the beat. That is when this Spice nation starts Playing Mas.

The sensory experience is overwhelming: the smell of oil and rum, the sound of drums echoing through narrow streets, and the sight of bodies painted in black, moving as one. This is living archaeology — a ritual that connects Grenadians to their ancestors through rhythm, costume, and spirit.

What Is Jab Jab

Our ancestors developed ways to surrender their bodies while retaining their humanity. Jab Jab is a cultural expression Grenadians created as an antidote to slavery and colonization.

The culture developed on French Caribbean plantations, where enslaved Africans sought spiritual solutions to brutality and erasure of their humanity. In Akan folklore, Anansi — the spider — embodied wisdom, survival, and strength through intellect over brute force. His stories taught that cunning and resilience could overcome oppression.

Banned from practicing African spirituality, slaves devised a mask to represent Anansi. They called it Jab Jab. What survived the Middle Passage, chattel slavery, and colonization is Jab Jab. We are still “Playing Mas” — in Grenadian patois meaning we are not the mask, we are just playing it.

Jab Jab Is We Story

The bedrock of a people is their culture and language. Jab Jab appeared in Grenadian patois during French plantation life. The Code Noir required Africans to be baptized Roman Catholic, and planters celebrated “Carna Vale” before Lent. Africans transformed this Roman festival into the Temple of Anansi, the wise trickster.

Forced Christianity and illiteracy blocked pathways to African spirits, so Grenadians played Mas — embodying the mask instead of making it. In patois: “We playing Mas” or “You playing man.”

This adaptation was survival. By disguising African spirituality within Carnival, Grenadians preserved their heritage under the watchful eyes of colonizers. Jab Jab became both resistance and resilience, a coded language of survival.

The Unbroken Spirit of Jab Jab

The spirit of Jab Jab survived slavery and now travels the world, worshipped internationally. Grenada remains the temple mound, and every J’ouvert morning we play Mas to celebrate Anansi and our ancestors.

Epigenetics teaches that memory is retained in DNA and recalled through generations. Our ancestors hid knowledge in a “treasure chest” for future generations. Today, Jab Jab presents to the world a celebration of survival through resistance and resilience — intellect over brute force.

Grenadians in the diaspora carry Jab Jab to cities like London, New York, and Toronto, where Carnival celebrations echo the rhythms of the Spice Isle. Jab Jab is no longer confined to Grenada; it is a global symbol of African survival and creativity.

Jab Jab and Grenada Carnival

Jab Jab is inseparable from Grenada Carnival. J’ouvert morning is the climax, when thousands of Grenadians take to the streets in oil, mud, and paint. The rhythm of Jab Jab drives Soca music, inspiring generations of artists and performers.

Carnival is not just entertainment; it is a cultural archive. Each chant, drumbeat, and costume tells the story of survival, resistance, and joy. Jab Jab is the heartbeat of Carnival, reminding Grenadians of their African roots.

Jab Jab and Grenada Tours

Today, Grenada Tours offer visitors a chance to experience Jab Jab firsthand. Cultural packages include J’ouvert participation, storytelling sessions, and visits to historic sites. Tourists learn that Jab Jab is more than a masquerade — it is a living tradition, a spiritual practice, and a cultural inheritance.

By promoting Jab Jab through tourism, Grenadians share their story with the world while strengthening local identity. Jab Jab becomes both a cultural export and a reminder of resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is Grenada Jab Jab?
A: Jab Jab is a traditional masquerade rooted in African spirituality, performed during Grenada Carnival. It symbolizes resilience, survival, and cultural identity.

Q: When does Grenada Carnival take place?
A: Grenada Carnival, also known as Spicemas, is held every August. J’ouvert morning features Jab Jab performances, Soca music, and vibrant parades.

Q: How can visitors experience Jab Jab?
A: The best way is to join J’ouvert morning celebrations. Many Grenada Tours offer cultural packages that include Jab Jab experiences.

Q: Why is Jab Jab important to Grenadian identity?
A: Jab Jab connects Grenadians to their African heritage, reminding them of survival through slavery and colonization. It is both cultural expression and spiritual resistance.

Conclusion

Jab Jab is more than a Carnival tradition. It is Grenada’s living link to Africa, a cultural inheritance passed down through generations. From the plantations of French colonization to the streets of modern Carnival, Jab Jab embodies resilience, creativity, and survival.

Grenada Jab Jab, Grenada Carnival, and Grenada Tours together showcase the power of culture to preserve identity and inspire the world.
